Why Rivers Meander

By A. Hydrologist · 9 min read

How a bend begins

Almost no natural river runs straight for long. A tiny disturbance — a fallen tree, a patch of softer bank, a sandbar — nudges the fastest water toward one side of the channel. That side erodes a little faster than the other, and the channel begins to curve.

On the inside of the curve the water slows down and drops sediment, building a gentle slope called a point bar. On the outside the faster current scours the bank, which becomes steep and undercut. Over years the whole bend migrates sideways across the valley floor.

The feedback loop

Once a bend exists it tends to grow. Water flowing around a curve develops a corkscrew motion called secondary flow: surface water is pushed toward the outer bank while water near the bed sweeps toward the inner bank, carrying sediment with it. The outer bank erodes, the inner bank builds up, and the curve sharpens.

This positive feedback would make bends grow forever if nothing stopped it. Eventually, though, the loop becomes so tight that the river cuts through the narrow neck of land between two bends, usually during a flood.

Oxbow lakes

When the river takes the shortcut, the abandoned loop is sealed off by sediment and becomes a crescent-shaped oxbow lake. Oxbow lakes slowly fill with silt and plants, turning into wetlands that are rich habitats for birds, amphibians and fish.

Geologists read old meander scars and oxbows like tree rings: they record where the river has been and how quickly its channel has moved across the floodplain.

Rivers and people

Meandering rivers deposit fertile soil across their floodplains, which is why so many early farming societies grew up beside them. The same restlessness makes them hard neighbours: a river that moves a few metres a year can swallow fields, roads and buildings.

Engineers once straightened many rivers to speed up shipping and drain land. Straightened channels often flood worse downstream, so restoration projects now give rivers room to meander again.
