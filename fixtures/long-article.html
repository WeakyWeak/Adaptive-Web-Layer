How central banks cool inflation, and why it takes so long

Explainer · 7 minute read

When prices rise faster than incomes, central banks are expected to act. Their main tool is the policy rate, the interest rate at which commercial banks borrow overnight from the central bank. In India this is the repo rate; in the United States it is the federal funds rate. Raising it by 25 basis points, a quarter of one percentage point, sounds small, but it ripples through every loan in the economy.

The ripple is called monetary policy transmission. A higher policy rate makes interbank lending dearer, so banks raise the rates they charge on mortgages, car loans and business credit. Borrowing falls, spending slows, and firms find it harder to pass higher costs on to customers. Eventually inflation eases. The catch is the transmission lag: economists estimate that the full effect takes between twelve and twenty-four months to appear in consumer prices.

Headline versus core inflation

Central banks watch two numbers. Headline inflation, usually measured by the consumer price index (CPI), includes everything a typical household buys. Core inflation strips out food and fuel, whose prices swing with harvests and oil markets. A central bank that reacted to every spike in vegetable prices would whipsaw the economy, so policymakers lean on core inflation to judge whether price pressure is becoming entrenched.

Entrenchment is the real fear. If workers and firms come to expect high inflation, they bargain for bigger wage rises and set higher prices in advance, and the expectation becomes self-fulfilling. This is why central bankers talk so much about anchoring inflation expectations: keeping the public confident that inflation will return to target, often around four percent in India or two percent in the United States.

Talking as a policy tool

Words can do some of the work that rate changes would otherwise do. Forward guidance is the practice of telling markets how the central bank expects to set rates in the coming months. If a governor signals that rates will stay high "for as long as necessary", long-term borrowing costs can rise immediately, before any further hike. Credibility matters here: guidance only works if markets believe the bank will follow through.

Markets read these signals through the yield curve, a chart of interest rates on government bonds of different maturities. Normally, lending for ten years pays more than lending for three months, so the curve slopes upward. When investors expect the central bank to cut rates later because a slowdown is coming, long-term yields can fall below short-term ones. This inverted yield curve has preceded many recessions, although it is a warning rather than a guarantee.

Shrinking the balance sheet

After the 2008 financial crisis and again in 2020, central banks bought huge quantities of government bonds to push long-term rates down, a policy called quantitative easing. Reversing it is quantitative tightening: the bank lets bonds mature without replacing them, or sells them outright. This drains liquidity, the ready cash in the banking system, and nudges long-term rates upward even when the policy rate is unchanged.

Central banks also manage day-to-day liquidity through open market operations, buying or selling short-term securities so that overnight rates stay close to the policy rate. These operations are routine plumbing, but when they fail, as in brief money-market squeezes, borrowing costs can spike for reasons that have nothing to do with inflation.

Real rates and the risk of overdoing it

What ultimately matters for borrowers is the real interest rate: the nominal rate minus expected inflation. A policy rate of six percent with inflation at seven percent is still loose, because money loses value faster than the interest it earns. Only when real rates turn clearly positive does policy start to restrain demand.

The danger is that, because of the transmission lag, a central bank that keeps tightening until inflation actually falls will have done too much by the time the full effect arrives. Overtightening can tip an economy into recession and raise unemployment. Policymakers therefore try to judge where inflation is heading, not where it is, and many now pause after a series of hikes to let earlier decisions work through.

None of this is precise. Supply shocks, exchange-rate movements and government spending all push prices around, and central banks see the economy only through data that arrive late and are often revised. Cooling inflation is less like turning a thermostat and more like steering a large ship: the wheel turns now, and the bow swings months later.
